2  Numerical methods

This chapter fixes the notation and the two time-stepping strategies used throughout. Individual application chapters refer back to it rather than restating it.

2.1 Reciprocal-space convention

Every spectral application is written in terms of the Laplacian symbol

\[ u \;=\; k_{\mathrm{lap}} \;=\; -|\mathbf{k}|^2 , \tag{2.1}\]

so that \(\widehat{\nabla^2 \psi} = u\,\hat\psi\) exactly. Using \(u\) rather than \(|\mathbf{k}|\) keeps every operator a polynomial with no square roots: \(\nabla^4 \mapsto u^2\), \(\nabla^6 \mapsto u^3\). It also makes the sign bookkeeping uniform — \(u\) is negative for every mode except \(\mathbf{k}=0\), which is where conservation lives.

A conserved equation is one whose evolution operator carries an explicit factor of \(u\). Because \(u=0\) at \(\mathbf{k}=0\), the mean of the field is then annihilated exactly, not to within a tolerance — a property several application chapters assert bit-exactly rather than numerically.

2.2 Spectral exponential time differencing

The spectral applications share one semi-discrete form. Splitting the right hand side into a part linear in the unknown and a remainder,

\[ \partial_t \hat\psi(\mathbf{k},t) = L(k)\,\hat\psi(\mathbf{k},t) + M_{\mathrm{nl}}(k)\,\widehat{N[\psi]}(\mathbf{k},t), \tag{2.2}\]

where \(L\) and \(M_{\mathrm{nl}}\) are pointwise multipliers and \(N\) is evaluated in real space. Integrating the linear part exactly over one step gives the first-order exponential time differencing (ETD1) update

\[ \hat\psi^{\,n+1} = e^{L\Delta t}\,\hat\psi^{\,n} + \frac{e^{L\Delta t}-1}{L}\,M_{\mathrm{nl}}\,\widehat{N[\psi^{\,n}]}, \tag{2.3}\]

with the coefficient evaluated by its Taylor series where \(|L\Delta t|\) is small enough for the quotient to lose precision.

The point of Equation 2.3 is that all of the stiffness sits inside \(e^{L\Delta t}\), which is a pointwise multiply and is unconditionally stable for \(L<0\) no matter how negative \(L\) becomes. A \(k^{10}\) operator is no less stable than a \(k^2\) one. The timestep is then limited by the nonlinearity and by accuracy, not by the highest derivative in the equation.

The practical size of that effect is worth stating concretely. On a grid with \(\Delta x = 2\pi/8\) the Nyquist wave number is \(k_{\max}=4\), and a sixth-order operator with unit coefficient has \(|L(k_{\max})| \approx 4^6 = 4096\), so forward Euler would need \(\Delta t < 2/|L| \approx 5\times 10^{-4}\). The corresponding tenth-order operator needs \(\Delta t \lesssim 2\times 10^{-6}\). ETD runs both at the timestep the physics wants.

One step therefore costs two transforms — a forward transform of \(N\) and an inverse transform of the updated field — plus \(O(1)\) work per mode.

2.2.1 Dealiasing

Where \(N\) is polynomial, the product raises the highest wave number present: \(\psi^2\) doubles it and \(\psi^3\) triples it. On a finite grid that content folds back onto resolved modes. Applications with a polynomial nonlinearity therefore apply the Orszag 2/3 rule, zeroing the upper third of the spectrum after each nonlinear evaluation. Purely linear applications (Chapter 7) do not need it and switch it off.

2.3 Finite differences

The finite-difference applications discretise derivatives with centred stencils of even order \(2m\), exchanging \(m\) ghost cells per face before each evaluation. OpenPFC provides the halo exchange for host and device fields, including a GPU-aware path that passes device pointers to MPI directly when the installation supports it.

Time stepping is explicit — forward Euler, or a Runge–Kutta scheme where the application needs it — and therefore subject to the usual parabolic condition

\[ \Delta t \;\lesssim\; \frac{\Delta x^2}{2 d\, D} \tag{2.4}\]

in \(d\) dimensions for a diffusion coefficient \(D\). This is exactly the constraint ETD removes for the spectral applications, and it is why the finite-difference applications in this report are second-order-in-space problems rather than sixth.

2.4 What “verified” means here

Each application chapter ends with a Verification section. The tests behind those sections fall into four kinds, and it is worth distinguishing them:

  • Analytical — the discrete operator is compared against a closed-form symbol or solution, typically to \(10^{-12}\) or better. This is the strongest kind and most of the spectral applications have one, because a single Fourier mode of a linear operator has an exact answer.
  • Conservation — a quantity the formulation should preserve is checked, in several cases bit-exactly.
  • Convergence — the error is measured against a refined or analytic solution across resolutions and the observed order compared to the design order.
  • Regression — a checksum is pinned so that a change of decomposition, backend, or platform is detected. These catch ports, not physics.

Where an application has only regression coverage for some property, the chapter says so.
