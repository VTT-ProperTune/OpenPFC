5  Cahn–Hilliard spinodal decomposition

apps/cahn_hilliard — conserved fourth-order phase separation, parameterised for the Fe–Cr miscibility gap.

5.1 Physical setting

An Fe–Cr alloy held at 475 °C inside its chemical spinodal separates spontaneously into Fe-rich and Cr-rich domains. There is no nucleation barrier: every long-wavelength composition fluctuation grows. The technological interest is that this is the mechanism behind “475 °C embrittlement” in ferritic stainless steels, and the length scale of the resulting microstructure sets the mechanical consequence.

5.2 Governing equations

One conserved composition field \(c\) (Cr mole fraction):

\[ \partial_t c \;=\; M\nabla^2\!\left( f'(c) - \kappa\nabla^2 c \right), \tag{5.1}\]

with the regular-solution bulk free energy in units of \(RT\),

\[ f(c) \;=\; \omega\,c(1-c) \;+\; c\ln c \;+\; (1-c)\ln(1-c), \qquad \omega = \frac{\Omega}{RT}. \tag{5.2}\]

The defaults \(T = 748.15\,\mathrm{K}\) and \(\Omega = 20.1\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) place Fe–32Cr inside the spinodal. This is a documented representative miscibility-gap model, not a CALPHAD assessment, and the report does not claim quantitative agreement with a thermodynamic database.

5.3 Spectral formulation

Linearising the bulk term about the alloy composition \(c_0\) puts \(f''(c_0)\) into the linear symbol and leaves the remainder to the nonlinearity. With \(u = k_{\mathrm{lap}}\),

\[ L(k) \;=\; M\big( f''(c_0)\,u \;-\; \kappa\,u^2 \big), \qquad M_{\mathrm{nl}}(k) \;=\; M\,u , \tag{5.3}\]

\[ N(c) \;=\; f'(c) - f'(c_0) - f''(c_0)\,(c-c_0). \tag{5.4}\]

Both channels carry the factor \(u\), so the mean composition is conserved exactly.

The growth rate follows directly from Equation 5.3. Writing \(|\mathbf{k}| = k\),

\[ \lambda(k) \;=\; -M k^2\big( f''(c_0) + \kappa k^2 \big), \tag{5.5}\]

which is positive — unstable — precisely when \(f''(c_0) < 0\), the spinodal condition. The fastest-growing mode is at \(k_{\max}^2 = -f''(c_0)/(2\kappa)\), and this wavelength is what sets the initial domain spacing.

The nonlinearity Equation 5.4 is not a low-order polynomial — it contains \(\ln\!\big(c/(1-c)\big)\) — so dealiasing is on.

5.4 Solution strategy

ETD1 as in Equation 2.3. Composition is clamped away from \(\{0,1\}\) so the logarithms stay finite; a sampled composition outside the physical range stops the run rather than producing quiet nonsense.

Optional collective CSV diagnostics record mass, composition bounds, and the current-state total energy including the spectral gradient contribution.

5.5 Verification

  • Analytical growth rate. A single seeded mode grows as \(e^{\lambda(k)t}\) with \(\lambda\) from Equation 5.5, matched within 5%.
  • Mass conservation. The mean composition is held to \(10^{-12}\) across a run.
  • Free energy decreases. The total free energy falls monotonically while a spinodal mode grows.
  • Gradient energy. The diagnostic gradient energy matches the analytical value on distributed grids.
  • Decomposition independence. Seeded noise produces the same field and the same mean on any number of ranks.

5.6 Binaries and inputs

cahn_hilliard (CPU), cahn_hilliard_hip. Presets: fe_cr_spinodal.json (single-mode verification) and coarsening.json (seeded broadband coarsening with diagnostics).

5.7 Scalability

Not measured. Shares the spectral ETD stack with Chapter 3.
